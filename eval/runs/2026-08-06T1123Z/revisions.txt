------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DRAFT:
The Oscarsborg Fortress played a crucial role during the German invasion of Norway in Operation Weserübung by firing on the leading German ship, the cruiser Blücher, at about 04:15 on 9 April 1940. The fortress's guns, including two 280 mm Krupp guns nicknamed Moses and Aron, inflicted heavy damage on the Blücher with artillery and torpedo hits, causing it to sink with the loss of 600 to 1,000 men. This action delayed the rest of the German naval invasion group long enough for the Norwegian Royal Family, Cabinet, members of Parliament, and the national treasury to be evacuated. The sinking of the Blücher and the threat from the fortress also caused the German cruiser Lützow to withdraw, delaying the arrival of the main German invasion force for Oslo by over 24 hours, although the city was still captured less than 12 hours after the loss of Blücher. This delay contributed to Norway never surrendering legally to the Germans, making the Quisling regime illegitimate and allowing the Norwegian government-in-exile to remain an Allied nation in the war [1][4].

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DRAFT:
Controversies around war memorials commemorating Aboriginal resistance to colonisation in Australia include debates on whether such memorials should be erected at all, as raised by historian Henry Reynolds in 1981. He questioned if Indigenous Australians who died fighting British invaders on their lands should be commemorated alongside other war dead, highlighting the tension between white Australians' desire to forget and the deep scars in black memories [2]. Memorials to Aboriginal resistance or massacres of Indigenous Australians have often generated controversy; for example, a 1984 memorial to the Kalkadoon people's resistance was frequently shot at and eventually blown up [1].
